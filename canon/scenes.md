## Dive 10: The Hum

### Narrator

Imre had the bowl turned up so loud that the children had stopped playing near it. She did not look up. She was counting on her fingers.

"Eleven," she said. "Listen."

The far house keeps its listening bowl open on the cold side. In my house the bowl sits behind a filter and gives us far-works and little else. Here it gives everything. What it gave was a hiss, like wind over a radiating face. I have heard it all my life without knowing I heard it. Read ears take it for silence.

Then it rose, slowly, the way a face brightens before waking. Imre let out her breath.

You asked what she was measuring. Every night the houses throw their heat at the dark, and anyone on the far limb can watch them do it. Imre has watched for forty years. She keeps two strips side by side: how brightly each house burned, night by night, and how loud the Hum was, waking by waking. For forty years they did not seem to match.

Then the east face let go, eleven wakings ago. That night Vane's house remade Tam and then Ket, and held Dov's reading, and burned brighter than it had in a generation. One sharp night in a long, even row. Imre marked it and waited.

Today the Hum is the loudest she has ever heard it.

"It isn't an echo," she said. "An echo gives you back what you sent. This gives back how much."

She slid one strip along the other by eleven wakings, and they matched. Not every rise. Most. The Hum, she says, is our heat, all of it, added up over several nights and handed back. Somewhere in the cold, near enough to answer in eleven wakings, something has been keeping count of what we burn. Most of what we burn is the cost of keeping us the same.

I said it might be dust. Heat can fall on a cloud and come back late and smeared. I said it because I can check nothing, and dust asks nothing of me.

"Then dust will give it back smeared," she said. "We'll see."

You asked who gave her leave. I think in your time someone had to. The throat governs standing on the road, and nothing else. Nobody governs heat. Any house may throw what it likes at the dark. It has never mattered, because nobody believed the dark was listening.

She did not ask the rails. She asked the house. By the half-waking thirty of them were on the radiating faces with the shutters, Maru among them, and Teo carrying a cup of water up the stair. They closed the faces and opened them in the shape Imre gave them: the swell she had just heard, given back. A read house could not do this. Every face it closes is a remaking it cannot afford. The far house keeps no ledger and has heat to spare.

So the first thing anyone in this region has said to the cold was said by a woman with no key, in the one language we know it hears.

Imre will not leave the bowl now. She says a reply in words, if it thinks in words, would take longer than she has. She has given the house her strips, to keep after her.

I left before sleep, as I must. In eleven wakings it will give back how much, or it will give back something else. I will not be there to hear which. I do not know which I would rather.

## Dive 9: Orrin's Handing

### Narrator

On the fourth waking Orrin had them carry him up the throat-stair in his sling, because the wall Pell needed was the one behind the throat, and Pell could not stay the night to read it slowly.

You asked why a dying man would climb. I think you expect a keeper to rest. A keeper cannot be remade while he holds the key, so every hour he spends is the only one. Orrin spent this one on a wall.

Pell put both hands flat on the comb and read a long time without speaking. I watched the cuts under Pell's fingers. To me they are what they have always been: short marks in rows, like the edge of a leaf. Above them are the keepers' names, nine lines of them back to the first nine. Those we can read.

Then Pell read aloud. Nine names, in no form I know. Then three lines, cut deeper and faster than the rest.

"Nine slept. Nine woke. Nine opened."

Pell read it twice, then went down the stair to be out of the house before the sleep, as every Unread visitor must.

So he had three lines, from someone who cannot be checked, about the first night the throat opened outward for the Standard.

Before the wall came the candidates. On the first waking, Asha, whom the release rail raised. On the second, Hesk, whom the hold rail raised. On the third, Ione, whom the Glossmakers raised in the cold house. Each sat by his bed a full waking and showed him nothing, as they were taught from infancy. Asha asked after his pain. Hesk asked what he wanted done with his strips. Ione asked nothing. She wrote, and her strips have two lines.

On the fifth waking the rails heard about the wall. The hold rail said what it had to say: a reading no one can check is a rumour with a reader. The Glossmakers asked Pell to sleep one night glossed, so the lines could be shared. Pell refused, as Pell always has. Orrin's walkers doubled at his door. One slept against it.

On the sixth waking Orrin sent for all three and asked each the same thing. Not what they would do at the throat. He knew they would not say. He asked where they would sleep.

Asha said: here. Hesk said: here. Ione said: where I have always slept.

He gave her the key. A hand on a wrist, a warmth, and a woman of thirty-eight who was nobody's keeper is one of nine, and an old man is only an old man.

You asked whether that means she will open the throat. I do not know. I do not think Orrin chose her for what she will do. I think he chose her because she is the only one of the nine whose mind the house will not smooth tonight. The other eight will lie down in read houses and wake, and believe what they believe.

Once he had handed on, Orrin was remakeable like anyone. He had them carry him to the reading room instead, and he lodged his refusal in person, as the law asks. His daughter stood at the door and did not stop him.

He died at the half-waking. The house will not make him again.

Ione went back to the cold house before sleep. Two walkers went with her. They will sleep there now, glossed, for as long as she lives.

I do not know what the nine said in the Before when they woke. I know only that they opened.

Standing in the way: the Glossmakers who raised her, walkers who would become mortal by following her, and hold extremists who would know exactly where an unguarded keeper sleeps.

## Dive 8: Custody and exchange

### Narrator

Three died on the east face at the half-waking when the frame let go: Tam, the apprentice Dov, and Ket, who had climbed up to see the light on the mat for her cloth.

The house can make two in a night. It has always been two.

You asked who pays for it. I think in your time you kept count of what people owed in tokens you could carry anywhere. We cannot carry heat anywhere. It is made here, it must leave here, and the house can throw only so much of it at the dark each night. Everything we do inside comes out of that: the reading, the remaking, the thinking itself. So we owe each other heat. You earn it on the faces, keeping the mat and the lichen clean so the house can shed more. You spend it by being expensive to keep.

The ledger is on the wall by the throat-stair. Nobody writes it. The house does.

Tam is owed a great deal, two hundred years on the east face. He goes first.

Dov is nineteen and owed almost nothing, but he is cheap. The house barely touches him at night. He is a plain young man, and his reading costs little.

Ket is in debt to her neck. Sixty years on one cloth, and every night the house smooths what she grew in the day, and smoothing is the hottest work it does. Ket has been the most expensive person in this house for longer than Vane has been alive.

So the order is Tam, then Dov. Ket's reading waits. The house can hold the reading of one dead person for a few nights before the heat of keeping it has to go somewhere. After that, there is nothing left to make her from.

Vane stood at the ledger a long time. Then she pledged. That is our word for putting your own standing behind someone else's. Her credit went to Ket, and Ket moved up, and Dov moved down.

Vane knew that would happen. Everyone at the wall knew. There is no pledge that only lifts.

They made Tam in the night, and then Ket. At waking Ket sat up with clean hands and was told her day: that she climbed to see the light for the cloth, that the frame went, that Vane had put her ahead of a boy. She asked which boy. They told her. She went to the loom and did not weave.

Dov's mother is on the east face now. She will work it through four wakings without sleep if her body lets her, because every hour on the face moves the ledger a little, and the house will hold her son's reading for four nights. Some of the scrapers have gone up with her. Some have not, because heat they earn for Dov is heat they will not have if they fall themselves.

You asked whether this is fair. I do not know what fair would be. The house did not decide it. The house only counts. It was built to count the cost of keeping us the same, and Ket costs more to keep the same than anyone.

Ilse brought Dov's mother water at the half-waking. Ilse will never be remade, by her own choice. She is the only one up there with nothing to lose by giving.

### Weakest call

A ledger that literally charges people for diverging could read as a thesis statement. It is kept because it follows mechanically from the heat limit and the Correction's cost, and because it gives the hold rail no easy defence. Later dives should show someone who defends it sincerely: in a house with fixed heat, someone has to come last, and the ledger at least is not a person choosing.
