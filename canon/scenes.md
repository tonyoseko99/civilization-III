## Dive 11: The relay engineers

### Narrator

Halde struck the housing twice with the flat of her hand, the way you wake a friend. Then she nodded to Sef, and Sef turned the wheel.

Nothing came down the road. Nothing has, for a long time. What went into the gate came from a sealed store in the floor of the housing, a pattern the wrights have never seen and call the blind. It went in from the solward side, as though the parent had sent it. We waited.

The gate rang once: a low note, felt more through the feet than heard. Refused.

Halde took a scraper from her belt and cut one short stroke into the housing below the last. The strokes run in rows up the casing, over the lip, and out of sight. One every thousand wakings since the Standard came. They are all the same length. None is missing.

You asked what a relay engineer is. In your time, I think, a person was given something for keeping a machine running, and went home. Nobody here is given anything for it; nothing could be given that would cross the house's wall. The gate is held in custody, like a strip box or a wall, and the wrights are its custodians. They sleep in the throat house, read like anyone, and are remade like anyone. Halde has died four times at the throat. She has not missed a test.

She asked me to come because the craft wants one witness from outside it. For 160,000 years that was a keeper. Since the Hold, keepers come with walkers, and walkers carry what they see to their rail. I carry it only to you.

"You will tell it plainly," she said, "and get it wrong. That suits the gate."

So, plainly. The gate decides what the road may put into us. It refused the blind today, as it has every thousand wakings. Three hundred and four years ago it did not refuse the retraction. It let it through the way it lets far-works through, because the retraction asks and does nothing. Halde was an apprentice then. She stood where Sef stood.

You asked whether the wrights know something the keepers do not. I asked her. She said the gate is the wrights' to keep, not to talk about, and that this was the rule before anyone had anything to say.

Sef walked me out. He is thirty-one and was fostered in a Glossmaker household, and he does not look at the housing the way Halde does. At the door he said, not to me, "If they knew, they'd stop arguing about the marks."

Halde was behind us. "If they knew," she said, "they'd stop arguing."

I have turned those two sentences over for six wakings. I think they are about the retraction. Only Sol could have given it teeth, and Sol chose not to. That is what most of us believe, and what I believe. Perhaps the wrights hold some proof of Sol's marks that would end the forgery quarrel. Perhaps they hold proof against them.

But Halde did not look like someone holding a proof. She looked like someone holding a door shut, and I could not tell which side she was afraid of.

The note stays in the housing if you lay your hand there. It fades over a waking. Sef keeps his hand on it until it is gone.

## Dive 10: The Hum

### Narrator

Imre had the bowl turned up so loud that the children had stopped playing near it. She did not look up. She was counting on her fingers.

"Eleven," she said. "Listen."

The far house keeps its listening bowl open on the cold side. In my house the bowl sits behind a filter and gives us far-works and little else. Here it gives everything. What it gave was a hiss, like wind over a radiating face. I have heard it all my life without knowing I heard it. Read ears take it for silence.

Then it rose, slowly, the way a face brightens before waking. Imre let out her breath.

You asked what she was measuring. Every night the houses throw their heat at the dark, and anyone on the far limb can watch them do it. Imre has watched for forty years. She keeps two strips side by side: how brightly each house burned, night by night, and how loud the Hum was, waking by waking. For forty years they did not seem to match.

Then the east face let go, eleven wakings ago. That night Vane's house remade Tam and then Ket, and held Dov's reading, and burned brighter than it had in a generation. One sharp night in a long, even row. Imre marked it and waited.

Today the Hum is the loudest she has ever heard it.

"It isn't an echo," she said. "An echo gives you back what you sent. This gives back how much."

She slid one strip along the other by eleven wakings, and they matched. Not every rise. Most. The Hum, she says, is our heat, all of it, added up over several nights and handed back. Somewhere in the cold, near enough to answer in eleven wakings, something has been keeping count of what we burn. Most of what we burn is the cost of keeping us the same.

I said it might be dust. Heat can fall on a cloud and come back late and smeared. I said it because I can check nothing, and dust asks nothing of me.

"Then dust will give it back smeared," she said. "We'll see."

You asked who gave her leave. I think in your time someone had to. The throat governs standing on the road, and nothing else. Nobody governs heat. Any house may throw what it likes at the dark. It has never mattered, because nobody believed the dark was listening.

She did not ask the rails. She asked the house. By the half-waking thirty of them were on the radiating faces with the shutters, Maru among them, and Teo carrying a cup of water up the stair. They closed the faces and opened them in the shape Imre gave them: the swell she had just heard, given back. A read house could not do this. Every face it closes is a remaking it cannot afford. The far house keeps no ledger and has heat to spare.

So the first thing anyone in this region has said to the cold was said by a woman with no key, in the one language we know it hears.

Imre will not leave the bowl now. She says a reply in words, if it thinks in words, would take longer than she has. She has given the house her strips, to keep after her.

I left before sleep, as I must. In eleven wakings it will give back how much, or it will give back something else. I will not be there to hear which. I do not know which I would rather.

## Dive 9: Orrin's Handing

### Narrator

On the fourth waking Orrin had them carry him up the throat-stair in his sling, because the wall Pell needed was the one behind the throat, and Pell could not stay the night to read it slowly.

You asked why a dying man would climb. I think you expect a keeper to rest. A keeper cannot be remade while he holds the key, so every hour he spends is the only one. Orrin spent this one on a wall.

Pell put both hands flat on the comb and read a long time without speaking. I watched the cuts under Pell's fingers. To me they are what they have always been: short marks in rows, like the edge of a leaf. Above them are the keepers' names, nine lines of them back to the first nine. Those we can read.

Then Pell read aloud. Nine names, in no form I know. Then three lines, cut deeper and faster than the rest.

"Nine slept. Nine woke. Nine opened."

Pell read it twice, then went down the stair to be out of the house before the sleep, as every Unread visitor must.

So he had three lines, from someone who cannot be checked, about the first night the throat opened outward for the Standard.

Before the wall came the candidates. On the first waking, Asha, whom the release rail raised. On the second, Hesk, whom the hold rail raised. On the third, Ione, whom the Glossmakers raised in the cold house. Each sat by his bed a full waking and showed him nothing, as they were taught from infancy. Asha asked after his pain. Hesk asked what he wanted done with his strips. Ione asked nothing. She wrote, and her strips have two lines.

On the fifth waking the rails heard about the wall. The hold rail said what it had to say: a reading no one can check is a rumour with a reader. The Glossmakers asked Pell to sleep one night glossed, so the lines could be shared. Pell refused, as Pell always has. Orrin's walkers doubled at his door. One slept against it.

On the sixth waking Orrin sent for all three and asked each the same thing. Not what they would do at the throat. He knew they would not say. He asked where they would sleep.

Asha said: here. Hesk said: here. Ione said: where I have always slept.

He gave her the key. A hand on a wrist, a warmth, and a woman of thirty-eight who was nobody's keeper is one of nine, and an old man is only an old man.

You asked whether that means she will open the throat. I do not know. I do not think Orrin chose her for what she will do. I think he chose her because she is the only one of the nine whose mind the house will not smooth tonight. The other eight will lie down in read houses and wake, and believe what they believe.

Once he had handed on, Orrin was remakeable like anyone. He had them carry him to the reading room instead, and he lodged his refusal in person, as the law asks. His daughter stood at the door and did not stop him.

He died at the half-waking. The house will not make him again.

Ione went back to the cold house before sleep. Two walkers went with her. They will sleep there now, glossed, for as long as she lives.

I do not know what the nine said in the Before when they woke. I know only that they opened.

## Dive 8: Custody and exchange

### Narrator

Three died on the east face at the half-waking when the frame let go: Tam, the apprentice Dov, and Ket, who had climbed up to see the light on the mat for her cloth.

The house can make two in a night. It has always been two.

You asked who pays for it. I think in your time you kept count of what people owed in tokens you could carry anywhere. We cannot carry heat anywhere. It is made here, it must leave here, and the house can throw only so much of it at the dark each night. Everything we do inside comes out of that: the reading, the remaking, the thinking itself. So we owe each other heat. You earn it on the faces, keeping the mat and the lichen clean so the house can shed more. You spend it by being expensive to keep.

The ledger is on the wall by the throat-stair. Nobody writes it. The house does.

Tam is owed a great deal, two hundred years on the east face. He goes first.

Dov is nineteen and owed almost nothing, but he is cheap. The house barely touches him at night. He is a plain young man, and his reading costs little.

Ket is in debt to her neck. Sixty years on one cloth, and every night the house smooths what she grew in the day, and smoothing is the hottest work it does. Ket has been the most expensive person in this house for longer than Vane has been alive.

So the order is Tam, then Dov. Ket's reading waits. The house can hold the reading of one dead person for a few nights before the heat of keeping it has to go somewhere. After that, there is nothing left to make her from.

Vane stood at the ledger a long time. Then she pledged. That is our word for putting your own standing behind someone else's. Her credit went to Ket, and Ket moved up, and Dov moved down.

Vane knew that would happen. Everyone at the wall knew. There is no pledge that only lifts.

They made Tam in the night, and then Ket. At waking Ket sat up with clean hands and was told her day: that she climbed to see the light for the cloth, that the frame went, that Vane had put her ahead of a boy. She asked which boy. They told her. She went to the loom and did not weave.

Dov's mother is on the east face now. She will work it through four wakings without sleep if her body lets her, because every hour on the face moves the ledger a little, and the house will hold her son's reading for four nights. Some of the scrapers have gone up with her. Some have not, because heat they earn for Dov is heat they will not have if they fall themselves.

You asked whether this is fair. I do not know what fair would be. The house did not decide it. The house only counts. It was built to count the cost of keeping us the same, and Ket costs more to keep the same than anyone.

Ilse brought Dov's mother water at the half-waking. Ilse will never be remade, by her own choice. She is the only one up there with nothing to lose by giving.

### Weakest call

A ledger that literally charges people for diverging could read as a thesis statement. It is kept because it follows mechanically from the heat limit and the Correction's cost, and because it gives the hold rail no easy defence. Later dives should show someone who defends it sincerely: in a house with fixed heat, someone has to come last, and the ledger at least is not a person choosing.

## Dive 7: the Before

### Narrator

Pell is a hundred and ninety, born unread in the second generation of the far limb. She is the oldest person alive who has never once been smoothed. She sits with her back to the house wall, and she does not need to turn round to read it.

The far-limb house is older than anything we have built. All of ours are. We live inside what was here before the Standard, and on every inner wall there are the cuts: rows of short grooves, close as teeth. In the read houses we call it the comb. Children run their fingers along it. I have slept beside comb all my life, and it has never looked like anything but comb.

You asked whether it is writing. Pell says it is. She reads it the way you read something you are tired of.

Most of it, she says, is lists. Who is owed heat, and by whom. Who slept where. Names, thousands of names, none of which any of us has ever heard. A long complaint about a neighbour who let his lichen spread onto someone else's face. She laughs at that part. I do not know why, and she does not explain. Maru says Pell's mother could read only the names. Pell reads most of it. Maru reads almost none. Behind me, Teo is pressing his whole hand flat against the comb as if it were warm.

I cannot check any of it. Nobody from a read house can. That is the first thing to understand about the Before. It is not lost. It is on every wall, in plain sight, and only the people we are losing can see it.

Low on the wall, near the floor, Pell found a line she had not shown anyone. It is newer than the rest, cut deeper and in a hurry. She read it to me twice. As near as I can carry it: *the ones who would not sleep for it have gone out to the cold, and they will be slow, and they will wait.*

The Glossmakers have asked Pell to spend one night in their house, so that the gloss can lay a plain version of the walls in her for everyone. She has refused. She says the gloss would make the walls say only what we can already say, and then everyone would think they had read them.

So I have a sentence, from a woman who cannot be checked, about a wall I cannot read.

I left before sleep. On the far limb they keep a listening bowl turned to the dark, for no reason anyone remembers. In our houses the same bowls feed a hum into the filters, and the filters take it out, as they take out anything without meaning. Here they let it run. It is very faint. It rises and falls once every few days.

I stood beside it until I had to go. I had never thought to listen to it. I could not tell you whether it is anything at all.

## Dive 6: the Gloss

### Narrator

There is one house in the region where a read woman and an Unread child can sleep under the same roof and both wake as themselves. It is small and cold, because it throws off less heat than ours, and the lichen on its shade side is thin. They call it the Glossed House. Its makers stand at the hold rail, mostly. They would say they stand at a third.

Vane came at the half-waking. Maru came from the far limb with Teo on her back. It was the first time the boy had met his grandmother, and the first night the three of them had ever slept anywhere together.

You asked what the house does instead. It reads, as ours does. But where ours smooths, this one writes. Whatever in you the Standard has no place for, it leaves alone, and beside it, it lays the nearest thing the Standard can say. The makers call that the gloss. You wake with your own thought and, next to it, a plain version anyone could follow.

I watched the waking. Teo said his word, the one for the moment before something falls, the way he says it to cups. And Vane, who had never once heard it right, said, "The moment before it falls." It was not his word. It was the gloss, which the house had laid in her in the night as well. It was enough. He looked at her as if she had finally learned to talk.

Maru saw it too. I watched her be glad, and then watch something else.

Then came the thing nobody had told her. The house reads, so it keeps. Somewhere in its cold wall there is now a reading of her son. He fell asleep a boy who dies once and woke a boy who could be made again. Vane knew this when she suggested the house. I think it was why she suggested it.

Later, while the grown ones talked, Teo found the strip-box by the door. Glossed strips have two lines, your own and the house's beneath it. He cannot read yet. He traced only the lower lines, all the way down the box. They are the even ones. They look like the writing grown-ups do.

The makers want the throat to speak, but not with Sol's message. They want it to send this: gentle, self-executing, carrying our marks, so that outward must accept it. Nothing smoothed, everyone glossed, every child remakeable, every far-work still read.

Maru asked the oldest of them what becomes of a child glossed from birth after nine generations. He said honestly that the house is forty years old and nobody knows. She asked whether they would wait nine generations before sending it. He said that every year they wait, the old Standard goes on smoothing the outward places, and they will not wait nine generations to stop that.

That is the argument the release rail makes, almost word for word. I do not think he noticed.

Maru took Teo home before sleep.

## Dive 5: template craft

### Narrator

There is a cloth on the loom-wall this waking that left its maker twenty-two thousand years ago.

It came as light down the road. The house rebuilds it thread by thread from the pattern, which takes a whole waking, and people come to watch it grow, the way you might watch weather.

It shows a heat-mat and a scraper, so the house it came from fed lichen too, or something like it. In the lower corner a small figure has dropped her scraper and is watching it fall, with a certain look on her face. When the corner finished, the whole room laughed at the same moment. Vane laughed, and so did I. The man next to me laughed. He has not laughed since his wife refused remaking and ended.

The weaver has been dead, or changed past knowing, for longer than this house has kept its tally. We understood her perfectly. You asked how we can be sure we read her right. We are sure. That is what the Standard was for, and it did it. A stranger made a joke for people who would not exist for twenty-two thousand years, and it landed.

Ket weaves for outward. She has been on one cloth for sixty years. It will go out by the small beam beside the throat, which needs no keys. Nothing she has sent has ever been answered, because nothing comes back up the road. It was not built to carry anything solward. She weaves for people she will never hear from, who may not be there. If they are, they will see her cloth long after she has ended, and they will know exactly what she meant.

She is not making anything new, if by new you mean a new kind of thing. Every part of her cloth is a kind of thing a weaver at Sol would have known. But nobody has made this cloth. She has put in the east face, Vane's grey arm, the tally-stone, and the throat with its warm rail. The arrangement is hers.

Today she asked me something she has not asked before. If the throat speaks and the places outward obey, how long before they cannot read her?

I told her what the far limb told me: nine generations, and then the words stop joining. A cloth may be slower than words. Perhaps they would see thread and colour for a while longer, and a figure watching something fall, and not know why it was funny.

She did not say which rail she stands at. She never has. She went back to the loom.

She is weaving faster now. I have heard her counting under her breath how many years the light takes to reach the next relay, and the one after. A cloth sent before the throat speaks will stay ahead of the retraction all the way out. It will land among people who can still laugh.

## Dive 4: Unread childhood

### Narrator

In the read houses, every child's first word is the same. It is the word for the one who answers when you call. I said it. Vane said it. As far as anyone knows, every child solward and outward of here has said it for a hundred and sixty thousand years. Nobody teaches it. It is simply the first thing the reading leaves alone.

This is the far limb. The light is wrong here, because they let the heat go where it likes and the walls sweat. That is Vane's daughter at the door. She has her mother's grey arm. She speaks slowly for me, the way you would for a child, which is kind and which I mind.

Her son is behind her. He is four. His first word was a sound that means, as near as she can tell me, the moment before something falls. Nobody taught it to him, and nobody had ever needed it before. He uses it for cups, and for his mother's moods, and for the lichen when it goes dry. Three other children on the limb use it now. None of their parents can.

You asked whether he is happy. I think you mean whether he is better off. I cannot see in him what the reading would have taken, and that is the whole trouble. I watch him lay out stones in a row, in an order I cannot find. He is not playing a game we have. He is making one. I have never seen a child do that. I did not know a child could.

At the side of the door there is a stone, smaller than Vane's tally-stone, with nothing cut in it. That was her first, a girl, who fell from the scaffold at three. In a read house she would have been sitting up the next waking, missing one day, and the Telling would have given it back to her. Here there was no reading to make her from. There is only the stone, and the dent in her mother's voice when she passes it.

I asked her the question the hold rail asks every Unread they meet. Would she give the outward places this, a dead girl and a boy who makes up his own words?

She said that in my house her daughter would have come back. Then she said that her daughter would have come back saying the first word.

She did not explain. She watched me not understand.

Then she told me the boy has started asking where his grandmother is. She cannot make him see that if they went to visit, they could not stay the night. To him sleeping is only sleeping. He does not know there are houses in this world where a night can take a child away from himself.

He will learn it from me, I suppose. I am the first mind still being read that he has seen up close.

I left before sleep. Everyone from the read houses does. On the way back I tried to say his word, and I could hear that I said it wrong.

## Dive 3: reconstruction

### Narrator

Tam fell from the east face yesterday, a little after the half-waking. The mat tore under him, and the drop is long. Vane found him. By the time the house had him, he was finished.

He is sitting up now. Look at his hands. They are clean. There was no lichen under the nails when he woke, because these are not the hands he scraped with yesterday. They were made in the night from what the house read of him the night before.

You asked whether he died. I think in your time that word closed a door. Here it closes nothing we can find. What we can find is a day. The house read him the night before he fell, so the man who woke is the man who went to sleep then. The waking in between is gone.

So we tell it to him. Everyone who saw any part of his day gives it back, in order, plainly. Vane gives the morning. He ate standing, he complained about the heat through the mat, and he said he would finish the east face before sleep. A man from the next house gives the noon. Vane gives the fall, and she does not soften it. Tam listens the way you would listen to news of a cousin. Then he nods and says he will finish the east face, and the room breathes.

He does not know he said that yesterday too. Vane does, and she has to sit down.

Not everyone is in the room. Ilse, who has shared his sleeping place for forty years, is out on the shade side. She comes of a line that holds the remade are someone new. The house does not smooth that out of her, because it can be said. She sat with him through the night while they made him, and she wept for the one who fell. At waking she went outside. She will come back, and she will be kind to him. She has done this twice before, and each time she was kind to someone she did not know.

The law here is simple. The last reading is the person. Whoever is made from it owns what they owned, owes what they owed, and is married to whom they were married. Ilse is married to him. Neither of them can be shown wrong.

This is what I watch. Vane's house still holds the last reading of her daughter, taken the night before she left for the far limb thirty years ago. They keep no readings out there. If the daughter ends there, the newest reading of her anywhere will be the one in this house, and by the law that reading is her. Vane could wake her: a woman thirty years younger who never left, who would remember the tally and the box of strips and nothing of the life that wrote the late letters on the wall.

Vane has not said what she would do. She reads the late letters more often now. Last waking she asked the house, aloud, whether a reading can be given up. The house did not answer. I do not think anyone has asked it before.

## Dive 2: the Correction

### Narrator

Vane writes before she sleeps. Watch her hand. She presses one line into a strip of dried lichen, slowly, and then she holds it out to me and to you.

Read it now, while it can be read. I will not say it back to you. I have learned that saying it does not help. It is about the east face, where she scrapes, and the heat coming up through the mat. Then the line turns, and there is something in the turn I have not met before. It is good. I can feel that it is good the way you feel a step that is not there.

Now we sleep. Everyone here sleeps at the same hour, flesh or not. I am told your people slept because their bodies made them. We sleep because that is when we are read. The house goes through each of us and compares what it finds with what a person is supposed to be. Where something has grown that the Standard has no place for, it is smoothed. Opinions are left alone. Vane's anger at the keepers is there at each waking, entire. What goes is whatever could not be said to a stranger forty thousand light-years away who was built the same as us. That was the purpose.

You asked if it hurts. No. You asked if she minds losing a thought overnight. You lose most of yours, I understand, and call it forgetting. Hers are selected.

It is the next waking. She takes up the strip. Look at her face. It is her hand, her east face, her heat through the mat. Then the turn, and after the turn they are all words she knows, and they do not join. I have the same trouble. I remember only that I admired it.

She puts it in the box. There are some nine thousand strips in the box. Most households keep one. Nobody reads them and nobody throws them out.

The reading is the hottest thing this house does. The lichen Vane scrapes lives on what the reading throws off. She knows this. She says she feeds the thing every day and it feeds on her every night, and that this is at least fair.

Her daughter left thirty years ago for the house on the far limb. They stopped the reading there when the retraction came, and they have slept unread for nine generations. Letters come. Vane keeps the early ones on her wall. The late ones have words in them she cannot follow, more each year. She cannot tell whether her daughter is becoming someone else or simply becoming someone.

Vane could go. But if she ever came back and slept one night here, everything she had grown there would be smoothed. She would have a box, and a daughter she could no longer follow. And the tally-stone is here.

She is looking at me. She is asking whether I still have it.

## Dive 1: the keepers

### Narrator

*\[Correction 2 applied: this scene was written in the first person of a keeper. It is re-attributed to the unnamed holdout keeper whom Dessa walks, speaking, with the Narrator observing and reporting. The Narrator holds no key (CHAR-0001). The words below are unchanged; every "I", "me" and "my" is the keeper's.\]*

Dessa eats first. She breaks the corner from whatever I am given, holds it in her mouth, and waits, looking at me the whole time. Then she passes me the rest. She has done this at every waking for eleven years and has never once wished me well.

She stands at the release rail. If the choice were hers, the throat would have spoken before she was born. But I carry a key, and a key cannot be copied. It is a cold seed under the skin of my wrist, and what is in it cannot even be looked at without being spoiled. It can be handed on once, whole, to one other. If I end before I hand it on, it ends with me. Then there are eight, and the throat wants nine. Nobody here knows how to make a ninth again. Whoever knew is forty thousand years solward.

So the people who most want me to change my mind are the people who most need me alive. Dessa's rail feeds me, walks me, and sleeps across my door. The danger comes from a few at my own rail. They have worked out that one dead keeper, any keeper, would settle the matter for good.

You asked why we do not count what everyone wants and do what the greater number say. I understand each word. I think your people did this when everyone a choice touched could be found and counted. The ones this touches most are outward, if they exist, and cannot be asked. And the key does not count anything. It opens or it does not.

What we have instead is the Handing. Orrin's is in six days. His body is finished, and he will not be remade, because whatever a remaking is, the seed will not follow it. He has stood with the hold for two hundred years. Three have been presented to him. They were raised for this from the time they could speak. One was raised by each rail, though neither rail admits it, and the third nobody can place. All three have been taught to show nothing. A candidate with a known heart gets refused by somebody. So the rails grow blank ones, and then cannot be sure what they have grown.

Orrin has not said which. He is not required to. The hold rail has started bringing him gifts, which they never did when they were sure of him.

Dessa thinks he has turned. I asked how she knew. She said he has begun reading the tally alongside Vane, under his breath. I had not noticed. I notice now.

If he hands to release, nothing opens. It still needs all of us. But there would be one voice fewer beside mine saying wait, and Dessa would still be breaking the corner off my portion, patient as the lichen.

I asked her once what she will do on the day I open it. She said she will stop tasting.

## Arrival (Phase 1A)

### Narrator

Keep your hand off the rail. It is warm, and it has been warm for three hundred and four years, since the throat behind it was brought to readiness and we could not agree to let it speak.

Look down the bore instead. At the far end of that dark is the aim point: the next relay, nine light-years outward. The keepers aim by light that left it nine years ago. It was there then.

*\[Correction 1 applied above. Original: "I aim by light that left it nine years ago."\]*

Vane is here already. She is the one by the tally-stone, with the grey arm. She is not a keeper. She tends the lichen on the shade side that eats our waste heat, and she comes here at every waking. She reads the tally aloud, though all of us know it. Then she looks at the keeper on watch, who does not open the throat, and she goes back to her lichen.

*\[Correction 1 applied above. Original: "Then she looks at me, I do not open the throat, and she goes back to her lichen."\]*

Three hundred and four. That is how many years we have added to the wait of every place beyond us.

You asked what we are holding. It is short. I can say it in two of your breaths.

*What we sent you was a mistake. Stop obeying it. Stop passing it on. Let what it changed in you go its own way. Pass this on instead.*

After that come the marks that say it is from Sol, and nothing else.

You want to know why that is hard. Everything here was made to the Standard, and I am too. The Standard came from solward as instruction, and we were built so that what arrived from solward became true in us. Nobody here was asked. The thing in the throat came down the same road, but it came without teeth. It asks. It is the first thing from solward that any of us could refuse.

If it is real, that is the proof they meant it: people who had learned what compelling does would not compel us again, even to undo it. If it is false, that is exactly how a false one would have to come, because only the makers could send one with teeth. And the marks are forty thousand years old. Anyone along the road has had time to learn them.

So consider what it asks. We are to trust the solward road one last time, in order to stop trusting it.

You asked what is happening out there now. I have turned that question over and I cannot make it bear weight. There is no now out there. There is what has already left here, travelling, and what has not. Under your question I think you mean: who is being hurt while I stand at this rail? I do not know that anyone is. The charts of what lies outward were old when they reached us. There may be ten thousand settled places past the next relay, each one still being rewritten year by year into something like me. There may be none. Vane counts as if they are there. The ones who stand at the far rail count differently. They say that if we speak, every place outward will drift loose, and in time nothing there will know us as people, and we will have made that dark on purpose.

You asked who has the right to decide. That is another strange one. The throat has nine keepers and opens only for all nine. Nobody chose us for wisdom. Each of us was handed a key by the keeper before, as far back as the Standard. Some would open it today. I hold no key. I only watch the ones who do.

*\[Correction 1 applied above. Original: "I am one of the ones who have not."\]*

You are the only mind I have met that was not made by the thing I am being asked to stop. So when Vane has gone, I will say the message to you again, slowly. I have said it to myself every waking for longer than she has been alive, and I cannot hear whether it was written by someone who was sorry.

Tell me what you hear.
